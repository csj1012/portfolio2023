Of course, I've been coding for much longer—since around 2000, when I built my own play-by-post RPG website. The narrative action would take place on hosted forums that one could customize using PHP templates that would, of course, render into HTML markup. The first of these was a table-based layout for a Pokémon RPG made in Paint Shop Pro. (I wanted to be the very best! Who didn't?!)

Nobody told me being a professional web developer was a thing until I was 19 years old, after I'd already been building and running my own online community for nine years. It was at this point that I started working at Spurlock Museum, a local museum on the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign campus. It was also around that time that I started to build and theme my first freelance Drupal sites (alongside the launch of CSS Zen Garden in 2009, and the paradigm shift that responsive design and web development brought to the industry).

Over the next 11 years I'd go on to build the Internet alongside some amazing folks at The University of Chicago, The American Medical Association, and later Last Call Media (an awesome fully distributed agency supporting higher education, nonprofit, government, and international brands). I've worn several hats throughout the myriad projects this decade brought, including as a designer, a developer, a project lead, and sometimes a glue work person-who-lives-and-breathes-the-project and probably needs to step away for a moment and take a deep breath.

The Web has been my life for the past 24 years. What used to be a private, anonymous place for weird nerds to build hobby pages and tight-knit communities has exploded into a world-shaking behemoth where specialized roles, frameworks, and entire industries abound. It is still, though, the single most important way humans can freely connect with one another and disseminate vital information around the world.

So what have I learned, in all this time—especially working professionally? Would I tell myself if I were starting out today? What would I like to tell *you?*

## The top four things I'd tell myself if I were just starting out now

### #1. You can learn The Thing

I know it has been hard to learn "The Thing." Maybe you even consider yourself "bad" at The Thing. You've never been able to be good at The Thing, even while others around you excelled at it.

What "The Thing" is is different for everyone: math, algorithms, CSS, lifting weights, public speaking. Different things come easily (or don't) to different people, and some people may just seem like they were born to excel at The Thing. They had advantages you don't have, however they got them, and whether or not it was fair.

That said: I am so happy to tell you that _The Thing is a skill like any other skill, and you can learn it._ Despite what you may believe about yourself, your innate abilities, and lack of current success, *it is possible for YOU*, too, to Learn The Thing.

Full post here with more of the "how:" LINK TO: You Can Learn The Thing

### #2. Frameworks come and go, but fundamentals are forever

I won't be adding any new thoughts to the zeitgeist by mentioning how many frameworks are out there, but what is more unique to me is perhaps my prior irritation at how quickly everything seems to move nowadays, and how I overcame it.

The main realization I've had over the past few years? Stop worrying, and start doing.

There were reasons why these frameworks were and still are developed, and *it is only natural that people are inventing new ways to build stuff on the Internet.* Opinions about this are healthy. But so is acceptance: there are very valid reasons why these frameworks were and still are developed, about as many reasons as there are unique use cases out there for digital products.

For me personally, the biggest savior amid the rising tide has been *my dedication to respecting Web Fundamentals.* If your HTML, CSS, JS, PHP (or your vanilla language of choice) is solid—if you know it inside and out, enough to understand how and why it's changed over time—you'll be better able to grasp new frameworks, or even decide you don't need them for your project at all. If you start looking into things and nothing makes sense to you, it may be that your fundamentals need attention!

Whatever it is, just start getting your hands dirty in something—anything—and I bet you'll feel less overwhelmed. More thoughts here: LINK TO: Frameworks Come and Go, but Fundamentals are Forever

Which brings me to...

### #3. Nobody will ever agree on how to build the Internet

For every ten people with Medium posts about the "so hot right now" framework-du-jour, there's ten more (okay, maybe two more) indie blogs talking about how We Will Never Need Frameworks Again.

Who's right? 

Probably both people! 

Who should you listen to? Whoever you want, or whoever's saying what you need to hear at the moment. The thing I want to caution against is taking one person's, or one school of thought's, word as gospel.

How many times have you seen a super popular thought online (maybe even that TikTok fridge organization hack where you put your veggies on the door of the fridge and condiments in the drawer)—and thought, "wow, these people have got it all figured out now!" And then a few months later, the rebuttal video makes the rounds to debunk everything?

I think this happens more in Web Dev world than in most online spaces. It's important to be able to find what drives *you*, find the new techs and paradigms *you're* interested in, and let that guide you. Define what *you* think is "good," be clear about that definition, and share it with others.

And if I haven't convinced you that *it's all going to be okay* yet...

### #4. On Dev Burnout

If you spend all your time working on things for other people—or things other people think you should be working on—you may forget the love of learning and building that hopefully brought you here in the first place. Be sure to make some time for yourself to revisit your roots, whether that's on the web or not. Maybe it's a Wayback Machine rabbit hole, looking back at old projects. Maybe it's reconnecting with old friends. Maybe it's picking up the instrument you learned as a middle schooler. Do something that brings you joy and helps build mastery, and allow yourself to play.

Humans thrive on meaningful connection, even the most introverted among us (ask me how I know). And one thing that connects many of us is that sometimes, we struggle. Talking to friends or peers—or getting your thoughts out on paper or in a text editor—moves the struggle out of your head temporarily and into some medium where it can be put aside, revisited later, analyzed (whatever works for you).

I know it's tough to find the time sometimes, but mentoring or pairing with others more junior than you (or even peers with similar but complementary skills) improves knowledge by leaps and bounds. Sometimes, if we're lacking motivation, helping others is enough.
