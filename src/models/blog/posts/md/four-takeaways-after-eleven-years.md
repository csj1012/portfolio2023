#### Introduction

##### The Internet I Grew Up With

Of course, I've been coding for much longer than the eleven years I've been working professionally as a dev—since around 2000, when I built a popular play-by-post RPG website.

Interestingly, nobody told me being a professional web developer was a thing until I was 19 years old, after I'd already been designing, coding, and administrating my online community for nine years!

In 2009, as an undergrad, I got my first job designing and coding websites and kiosks at a local museum on the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign campus.

It was also around that time that I started to build and theme my first freelance Drupal sites (alongside the launch of <a href="https://csszengarden.com/">CSS Zen Garden</a> in 2009, and the paradigm shift that responsive design and web development brought to the industry).

Over the next 11 years I'd go on to build web projects alongside some amazing folks at <a href="https://www.uchicago.edu/en">The University of Chicago</a>, <a href="https://www.ama-assn.org/">The American Medical Association</a>, and later <a href="https://latcallmedia.com/">Last Call Media</a> (an awesome fully distributed agency supporting higher education, nonprofit, government, and international brands). 

I'd worn several hats across the myriad projects this decade brought: a designer, a developer, a project lead, and often a person-who-lived-and-breathed-the-project and probably needed to step away for a moment and take a deep breath.</p>

<p class="pull">The Web—and building stuff for it—is my number one passion and pastime, and has been for the past 24 years.

Today, it is a different animal.</p>


##### The Web has Changed A Lot

<p>What used to be a private, anonymous place for weird nerds to build hobby pages and tight-knit communities has exploded into a world-shaking behemoth where specialized roles, frameworks, and entire industries abound.</p>

<p>That said, it remains the single most important way humans can freely connect with one another and disseminate vital information around the world—and because of that, I will always love it.</p>

So what have I learned, in all this time—especially working professionally? What would I say to myself if I were starting out today? 

What would I like to tell *you?*


#### The top four things I'd tell myself if I were just starting out now

##### #1. You can learn The Thing

I know it has been hard to learn The Thing. 

Maybe you even consider yourself "bad" at it, whatever it is. You've never been able to be good at The Thing, even while others around you excelled at it.

What "The Thing" is is different for everyone: math, algorithms, CSS, lifting weights, public speaking. 

Different things come easily (or don't) to different people, and some people may just seem like they were born to excel at The Thing. Or maybe they had advantages you don't have, however they got them, and whether or not it was fair.

That said: I am so happy to tell you the following: 

<p class="pull">The Thing is a skill like any other skill, and you can learn it.</p>

Yes! Seriously! Despite what you may believe about yourself, your innate abilities, and lack of current success, *it is possible* for you (yes, you!) to Learn The Thing.

Full post here with more of the "how:" <a href="/#/blog/you-can-learn-the-thing">You Can Learn The Thing</a>

##### #2. Frameworks come and go, but fundamentals are forever

I won't be adding any new thoughts to the zeitgeist by mentioning how many frameworks are out there, but what is more unique to me is perhaps my prior irritation at how quickly everything seems to move nowadays, and how I overcame it.

The main realization I've had over the past few years? Stop worrying, and start doing.

There were reasons why these frameworks were and still are developed, and *it is only natural that people are inventing new ways to build stuff on the Internet.* 

Opinions about this are healthy. But so is acceptance: there are very valid reasons why these frameworks were and still are developed, about as many reasons as there are unique use cases out there for digital products.

For me personally, the biggest savior amid the rising tide has been a dedication to respecting Web Fundamentals. 

If your HTML, CSS, JS, PHP (or your vanilla language of choice) is solid—if you know it inside and out, enough to understand how and why it's changed over time—you'll be better able to grasp new frameworks, or even decide you don't need them for your project at all. 

<p class="pull">If you start looking into things and nothing makes sense to you, it may be that your fundamentals need attention.</p>

Whatever it is, just start getting your hands dirty in something—anything—and I bet you'll feel less overwhelmed. 

More thoughts here: <a href="/#/blog/frameworks-come-and-go-fundamentals-are-forever">Frameworks Come and Go; Fundamentals are Forever</a>

Which brings me to...

##### #3. Nobody will ever agree on how to build the Internet

This used to stress me out *so much*.

For every ten people with Medium posts about the "so hot right now" framework-du-jour, there are often ten more (okay, maybe two more) indie blogs talking about how "We Will Never Need Frameworks Again."

Who's right? Probably both people! 

The thing I want to caution against here is taking one person's, or one school of thought's, words about how build stuff on the Internet as fact.

Even outside the world of web dev, how many times have you seen a super popular thought online (maybe even that social media fridge organization hack where you put your veggies on the door of the fridge and condiments in the drawer)—and thought, "wow, these people have got it all figured out now!" And then a few months later, the rebuttal video makes the rounds to debunk everything? Just me?

I think this happens more in our industry than in most online spaces. There is social and financial incentive to "disrupt." 

Amid so many voices, it's important to be able to find what drives *you*, find the new techs and paradigms *you're* interested in, and let that inspiration guide you. 

<p class="pull">Define what *you* think is "good," be clear about that definition, and share it with others.</p>

More thoughts: <a href="/#/blog/people-will-always-disagree-about-how-to-build">People Will Always Disagree About How To Build Things</a>

And if I haven't convinced you that *it's all going to be okay* yet...

##### #4. On Dev Burnout

Many of us spend all our time working on things for other people—or things other people think we should be working on.

For me, it can be too easy to forget the love of learning and building that brought me here in the first place (the kid building online roleplaying games). 

If this resonates, please be sure to make some time for yourself to revisit your roots, whether that's on the Web or not. 

Maybe it's a Wayback Machine rabbit hole, looking back at old projects. Maybe it's reconnecting with old friends. Maybe it's picking up the instrument you learned as a middle schooler. 

<p class="pull">Do something that brings you joy and helps build mastery, and allow yourself to play.</p>

Also: I know it can be hard, but it's vitally important to reach out to other people if you're having a hard time.

Humans thrive on meaningful connection, even the most introverted among us (ask me how I know). 

And one thing that connects many of us is that sometimes, we struggle. Talking to friends or peers—or getting your thoughts out on paper or in a text editor—moves the struggle out of your head temporarily and into some medium where it can be put aside, revisited later, analyzed (whatever works for you).

And lastly: I know it's tough to find the time sometimes, but mentoring or pairing with others more junior than you (or even peers with similar but complementary skills) improves knowledge by leaps and bounds. 

<p class="pull">If we're lacking motivation, reaching out to and helping others can often be enough to get us unstuck.</p>


More thoughts here: <a href="/#/blog/on-dev-burnout">On Dev Burnout</a>

#### Looking ahead

What's got me excited these days?

- New developments in design systems re: designer collaboration, accessibility, and reusability
- Continuing my deep dive into OOP fundamentals/expanding skills lower down the stack
- New developments in CSS, making styling more fun than ever before
- The return of free and indie web ideals, toward the goal of free connection and dissemination of information
- Exploration into cool ways to tell stories on the Internet

<p class="pull">I'd tell anyone starting out today to remember that now more than ever, being a developer means more than just writing code: it's finding what inspires <em>you</em>, building your community, and learning to take a step back and do what works for you.</p>
