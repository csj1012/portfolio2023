### You have to be constantly learning, forever

#### Frameworks come and go

When I started out in 2000, table layouts were the hotness. You'd make a full image of your layout and code it up in an HTML table, with `overflow: auto;` scrollable areas for content. These layouts couldn't dream of being responsive (which wasn't really a concept yet, even though the original web was and still is responsive.)

Then there was CSS Zen Garden. Then atomic design. Then design systems.

I haven't even mentioned front-end JS frameworks like React, Angular, Vue, Next, Nuxt, Narfxt (okay, that one's made up)

I used to be salty but now it's ok, people like to experiment and iterate on their use cases. There were reasons why these frameworks were and still are developed.

And it's absolutely worth your time to explore them and evaluate whether or not they work for you. Does this mean you have to jump from buzzword to buzzword, learning whatever you think "employers are looking for?" No. The important thing is to pick something that intrigues you, explore it, and get your hands dirty in it. Compare it to other things you know.

Once you've done this a bunch, picking up new frameworks is easy, and the constant evolution (while there are some issues with it, which mayn others have already written about so I won't) feels less daunting.

#### Fundamentals are forever

image of twins from the shining

LOTR elves having eternal lives so they can maintain strong fundamentals

Nothing I've described above happens if you don't have strong fundamentals. I don't say that pejoratively, but simply as a fact: *if you don't know the fundamentals that everything's based on: in front end world, how browsers request, receive, and render web pages using HTML, CSS, and JavaScript*, your life will be much harder because *nothing else you try to learn will make sense*.

When I first got into working in React in the real world (outside of building my two-portfolios-ago site in it by following a tutorial after going "I should learn this"), I was in for an unpleasant surprise: there were all these rules that didn't make any sense. How was anyone supposed to just rote memorize this? 

Turns out my JavaScript (vanilla) fundamentals were lacking. Yes, I could do a lot of things in JS, but I didn't fully understand why I was doing them, or how they worked. So, like I described above, I got down to business relearning the basics, after having come up alongside jQuery (which existed solely to manipulate the DOM and was really good at it, albeit clunky by our modern understanding). Why get better at vanilla JS when there's jQuery, right? (18-year-old-——me, probanly)

The thing was, *I didn't know what I didn't know.* Helpful mentors, colleagues who were more experienced than I was, and many hours of reading others' experiences on social media platforms helped me massively with that.

Don't knock your fundamentals. You won't be able to do the fancy stuff without them.
