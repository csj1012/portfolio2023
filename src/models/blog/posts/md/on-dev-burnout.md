I'm very vocal about the idea of burnout. 

I believe that the more transparent we can be with one another about how we're doing is vital to our success as developers; contrary to popular belief and stereotype, we are not people who can "just shut off our emotions" and write code.

I also believe that when we don't speak about our burnout to anyone, it gets worse. 

I have a lot more thoughts about this, so maybe someday I'll make a follow up, but here are some ideas that work for me when I find myself feeling the dreaded, familiar feeling we all know so well:

#### Ways to restore your passion for web dev

##### Revisit your roots from time to time

My roots are in play-by-post RPGs, the likes of which were popular in the early 2000s on. (I talk about this a lot, but as you'll see, it's very important to me.)

This is how I got my start, as a kid: I'd imagine the world I wanted to create for other people to experience, the settings the characters would interact in, the lore... and I'd design a (at the time, table-based) layout in Paint Shop Pro (or later, _definitely not pirated_ Photoshop). 

And then I'd build it. 

There were no best practices on my radar then, except those for writing RPG forum posts, and for running my RPG as a community. 

I had my own strict visual standards after stalking (and participating in) forums where people would roast other people's designs. I wonder if any of those people suspected the person on the other end of the monitor was a literal child telling them their color scheme was too discordant and their text was too hard to read.

These days, Modern Chelsie is very careful to document every function choice, architecture decision, and general thought. This is how good documentation is written; how salient, cohesive git logs are committed to history; this is how JIRA tickets are loaded with relevant detail for any future friends (or foes) needing to debug things. 

But sometimes...

<p class="pull">Sometimes you just have to go with the flow. Follow the passion and hyperfocus and *sheer joy* that comes with allowing creativity to run wild. Whatever that means to you.</p>

As for me, I've considered rebuilding my old RPG recently, using modern tools, frameworks, and best practices. 

Have I done any analysis, competitor research, serious planning toward this goal? *No way!* I simply opened VSCode on a whim one night, created a file called `rpg.js`, and started writing out classes. 

Will I even finish it? Who knows. Will I change it later? Probably. 

Is it worth it anyway? Absolutely, 1000%, yes. 

Because prior to that, I'd been feeling utterly devastated at the state of the tech industry, at seeing online heroes I'd looked up to for a decade being laid off and struggling to find work... and this got me coding again.

If you spend all your time working on things for other people—or things other people think you should be working on—you may forget the love of learning and building that hopefully brought you here in the first place.

#### Look at abstract disciplines that relate

I'll offer three examples, based on my own hobbies: performing in plays and musicals, making things with my hands (sewing and knitting my own clothing), and attempting to wrangle nature. 

As it turns out, everywhere you look, there are parallels to be found between coding and other arts:

- **Performing arts:** Facing rejections from job hunting? What's the big deal; performers face rejection all the time! More transferrable skills include: improving public speaking skills/being in front of a crowd, learning to work seamlessly with others under immense pressure
- **Sewing my own clothing:** Sewing patterns are one big documentation instance, for better or for worse. Interpreting the author's intent, highlighting gaps in understanding, and consulting peers and the Internet to supplement your knowledge? Figuring out how all the pieces fit together correctly? That has a lot of parallels to web dev, doesn't it?
- **Knitting garments and accessories:** If sewing patterns are documentation, knitting patterns are algorithms. `x` series of stitches in `y` configuration times `n` equals a certain shape. Enough of those shapes and you have a sweater. Much has been written online about <a href="https://opensource.com/article/18/6/how-programming-evolved-knitting">programming and knitting</a>, and that's before we look at knitting machines (the practical physical application of the intersection of these two concepts).
- **Gardening:** Systems, systems everywhere! The marigold you planted four years ago whose seeds keep popping up everywhere? If you leave the volunteer plants, they'll help repel pests from your veggies. But they'll also crowd out shorter plants, so it's lnecessary to thoughtfully manage them. Things affecting other things in the future that you might not anticipate, so you have to try and think ahead using established patterns? Design systems, anyone?

Maybe this sounds goofy, but I find inspiration for coding (and thinking about coding) in all of my disciplines outside work, and often find programming breakthroughts in the pursuit of excellece in other arts.

### Make time to be human and find balance

#### Force yourself to take breaks!

If I haven't talked your ear off about the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pomodoro_Technique">Pomodoro Method</a> for managing focus and productivity, suffice to say I've been a big fan of this concept for about a decade now. 

I use the Focus Keeper app currently, but there are free alternatives out there, too. The gist is that you work for like 25 minutes (or however long you want), then take a 5-minute break, then repeat. 

Seems simple, but it's actually vital to force yourself to take breaks for physical and mental health (no matter how close you are to "almost fixing the bug"):

Taking breaks has physical benefits, helping you avoid:
- Eye strain
- Brain fog
- Fatigue
- Muscle tightness 

As well as mental benefits:
- Renewed clarity after stepping away for a moment
- A "reset" for frustrated emotions
- And, as a bonus for anyone relying on other developers for help: one Pomodoro instance is a great timer for "try solving the problem yourself before going to the person helping you" without spinning your wheels for hours! 

#### Be honest: talk about (or write down) your struggles and mistakes

Ask yourself, which internal monologue feels worse to imagine: 
1. "I have no idea how to solve this complex problem, I'm a failure"
2. "I have no idea how to solve this complex problem, I'm a failure, and *I can't tell anyone about it, so I'm also a fraud*"

Even just getting your thoughts down on paper (or text editor of choice) goes a long way for me, personally.

<p class="pull">It is very, very hard to do great work when you're in your head. </p>

If you talk to peers about it, you'll likely find that others have felt the exact same way, and might even know what to do about it (keep in mind, it's worth figuring out the right audience for this—whether you choose anonymity online, finding a peer in your workplace, a trusted mentor, etc.).

But even without a solution, writing down the problem can be critical to your well-being. At least then it can live somewhere else than inside your head.

### When lost, help others

#### Paying it forward

Mentoring junior developers is so highly rewarding. 

I have been helped by lots of really smart and kind people throughout my career, and I'm fortunate to be able to pay it forward by doing the same for others whenever I can.

<p class="pull">If you feel bored, hopeless, or burned out, try coming to the aid of someone who still has the passion you're looking for, and you might find it renewed. </p>

Or, it might help your mind break out of a self-defeating rut, depending on the reason for your burnout. 

When I was younger, I was always mystified at how people found the time to mentor others while also keeping their own boat afloat... and while that is certainly a real challenge, I've realized by now that you *make time for it because it helps keep your own boat above water.*

Maybe that's selfish, to the extent that any altruism can be? I don't know. I just know that I like helping other people, and I find that it often renews my energy and motivation.

#### Solidify your knowledge; widen your perspective

Teaching also forces you to figure out how to explain things in various ways, to people who may have different backgrounds or levels of understanding than you do. 

This can broaden your perspective and improve your problem solving skills.

Several years ago, I briefly had the opportunity to volunteer teaching ballet to absolute beginner dancers. When teaching them a dance for a performance, we were all feeling demoralized; there was a common step, used everywhere in the dance, that they were having a hard learning. 

After a little while, I realized the reason: some of them had never learned to count music (a skill which I'd taken for granted, having been playing music and dancing from an early age). That realization helped me take a step back, meet the dancers at their level of understanding, and move forward with them together. 

Once everyone understood why I kept yelling out "brush on three," everything fell into place, and the dancers were able to give a performance they were proud of. 

<p class="pull">For me, this was a fundamental lesson: be curious about the details of how others learn, and where they're coming from. </p>

I've found that this skill, honed via working very closely with lots of stakeholders of varying technical experience over the past decade, has helped me improve relationships with clients beyond a level many developers think is possible.

Teaching also highlights gaps in your knowledge. If someone asks you "why?" and you don't have an answer, now you know what you don't have an answer to! And that's very, very valuable! 

#### Final thoughts

I won't be coy about saying that the best way to combat burnout is to take a break, short or long. 

I took nearly a year off from mid-2022 through mid-2023 before I started diving headfirst into learning JS fundamentals again (during that time I was volunteering and doing a ton of theater, so it wasn't like I was sitting around, but it was an intentional sabbatical from the industry). 

I needed to decide if I still wanted to build websites after 24 years, and taking time off allowed me to realize that, yes, I do, and with a renewed passion that I have to be careful to take breaks from sometimes :)

Not everyone has the privilege to take time off. I'm fortunate that I did. 

If you do not, for whatever reason, I hope that this post can help at least one person find some breathing room amid a career landscape that can often feel so overwhelming and all-consuming as to be stifling.
