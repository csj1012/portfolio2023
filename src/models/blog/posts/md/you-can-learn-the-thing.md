#### Everything is a skill

Outside of building stuff for the web, I act in plays and musicals. I've been doing this all my life (much like coding), having taken my first dance lesson at 3, and started singing around that time. But in college I was cut from a BFA acting program. I wasn't experienced enough in life yet, and didn't know enough about myself yet, to really connect to the curriculum.

What does this have to do with web development? 

When I was unable to get much from the acting program there, and was subsequently cut from the program, I internalized that I was "bad at acting" and that I would forever be "bad at acting." You were either born with it or you weren't, and I wasn't.

Turns out that's not true.

I returned to acting classes—ones that I found my footing in, and most importantly where I felt comfortable asking for what I needed explained in different ways that made sense to my brain—and as it turns out, *acting is a skill.*

And as it further turns out, *everything is a skill.*

Singing. Math. Coding. Riding horses. Juggling. Making friends. Needlepoint (you get the picture). *Everything is a skill and can be learned.* Will you be a started-in-childhood "genius" praised by everyone for your natural prowess? Maybe not. And that is okay.

Talent is the intersection of experience and hard work. Or something. Find the actual quote here. It's not necessarily something you're born with, and even if you are, it's not much without training and discipline to refine it.

I've now spent many hours working on my acting skill and feel confident and have been told I'm "good," and while I gave up on any dreams of stardom at age 19, the ability to improve is fulfilling beyond measure.
#### Learning how to learn

When I applied this thinking to programming and increasing my back end proficiency (as well as front end modern practices, frameworks, etc.)—my skill, talent, and confidence improved by leaps and bounds.

I'm not the same developer I was ten years ago, or even one year ago. And that's a wonderful thing.

I've documented my learning process (which I'll make a blog post about soon), learned my style (auditory, visual, kinesthetic—most people are a combo of both, maybe it's like a triangle where certain points are more dominant). 

I also learned to document my thoughts *during the learning process*, which has been extremely eye opening. I do this by keeping a Dev Log, which is essentially a giant journal in Obsidian. I write down everything I'm doing while I'm coding, and why. This allows me to keep my thoughts straight, not lose track of goals or "what I'm trying to do" when things get too complex... but it also allows me to jot down the "I don't have to properly learn this" or "I'm afraid of this concept" thoughts that sneak in and aren't obvious to me unless I consciously write them down. And once I do? Sometimes, they go away. Or at least I can poke holes in them. I plan to write a post about this soon, too.

### #### Figure out what you're good at

##### So you can find your niche

The world of web dev is so vast these days, with so many frameworks, tools, and best practices out there.

If you've received good feedback from a colleague about a particular skill you have, lean into that.

For example, a colleague of mine remarked that I'm great at looking at the UIs I build with a designer's eye—as a result, he knows he can trust me to deliver something great, and we can truly collaborate. 

Learning about this skill of mine has helped me figure out my role in tech.

##### So you can know what to improve

Nobody is good at everything. Full-stack development seems like a loaded term these days. What makes a good full-stack developer? We come in so many different flavors.

It can be helpful—and very empowering—to identify your weak spots, though it may feel impossible at first. 

Somewhat strangely, it was ChatGPT that helped me out with this one time, a year ago: 

"Why am I going in circles?"
GPT: "Learn this, this, and this."

So I did! I picked up CLASS A and CLASS B. I devoted hours per day to structured learning via these courses, using GPT to fill in the gaps when I had questions about something in more detail (of course, I would always double check its answers, cross-referencing with documentation, blogs, official course material, and other online sources.)

As a result, I'm a much more confident developer because:
- I know I can learn things I missed learning growing up (having taught myself to build websites without formal education) and I have solid process for holistic problem solving
- I have better foundational programming skills, data structure/algo knowledge, etc., which not only aid in my ability to perform front end tasks, but also help me gain footing in the many frameworks that are prevelant today (and were written by people with traditional CS backgrounds/lower-level progarmming backgrounds)
