Do you have something in your life that you just *know* you'll never be good at? 

Cool, this post is for you.

#### Everything is a skill

Outside of building stuff for the web, I act in plays and musicals. I've been doing this all my life, much like coding, starting with my first dance lesson and singing lessons around age 3.

But in college, I was cut from a BFA acting program, partly because I wasn't experienced enough in life yet to fully benefit from the training.

What does this have to do with web development? 

When I was cut from that program, I internalized that I was "bad at acting," and that I would forever be "bad at acting." Everyone likes making fun of actors who are bad at acting, right? You were either born with it or you weren't, and I wasn't. 

Turns out that's not true.

It took me ten years, but on a whim one day, I returned to acting classes—ones that I found my footing in, and most importantly where I felt comfortable asking for what I needed explained in different ways that made sense to my brain.

As it turns out, *acting, like most things, is a skill.*

And as it further turns out, *everything is a skill.*

<p class="pull">Singing. Math. Coding. Writing algorithms. Juggling. Making friends. Needlepoint (you get the picture). Everything is a skill and can be learned.</p>

Will you be a 'child prodigy,' praised by everyone for your natural prowess? Maybe not. And that is perfectly fine.

Talent is the intersection of time spent, training, and skill. It's not necessarily something you're born with, and even if you are, it's not much without discipline (and ideally guidance) to refine it.


#### Learning how to learn

When I applied this thinking to programming and increasing my back end proficiency (as well as front end modern practices, frameworks, etc.) my confidence and ability improved by leaps and bounds.

<p class="pull">I'm not the same developer I was ten years ago, or even one year ago. And that's a wonderful thing.</p>

I've documented my learning process (which I'll make a blog post about soon), familiarized myself with my unique learning style (auditory, visual, kinesthetic—most people are a combo of the three with differing dominant points). 

I also learned to document my thoughts *during the learning process*, which has been extremely eye opening. 

I do this by keeping a Dev Log, which is essentially a giant journal in Obsidian. I write down everything I'm doing while I'm coding, and why. This allows me to keep my thoughts straight, not lose track of goals or "what I'm trying to do" when things get too complex... 

And it also allows me to jot down the "I don't have time to properly learn this" or "I'm afraid of this concept" or "I'm not good enough" thoughts that sneak in and aren't obvious to me unless I consciously write them down. 

And once I do write them down? Sometimes, they go away. Or at least I can poke holes in them. I plan to write a post about this soon, too.

#### Figure out what you're good at

##### So you can find your niche

The world of web dev is so vast these days, with so many frameworks, tools, and best practices out there.

If you've received good feedback from a colleague about a particular skill you have, lean into that.

For example, a colleague of mine remarked that I'm great at looking at the UIs I build with a designer's eye—as a result, he knows he can trust me to deliver something great, and we can truly collaborate. 

Learning about this skill of mine has helped me figure out my role in the tech landscape, and while I'm always interested in both expanding and deepening my knowledge, knowing where I'm starting helps me chart out where to go next.

##### So you can know what to improve

Nobody is good at everything. Full-stack development sometimes feels like kind of vague term these days: what makes a good full-stack developer? We come in so many different flavors!

It can be helpful—and very empowering—to identify your weak spots, though it may feel impossible at first. 

Somewhat strangely, it was ChatGPT that helped me out with this one, a year ago: 

<blockquote class="chatgpt-conversation">
    <div class="chatgpt-message user">
        <p><strong>Chelsie:</strong> "I'm having issues programming in JavaScript: I keep coding in circles. Why do I suffer from so much confusion and circular logic?"</p>
    </div>
    <div class="chatgpt-message gpt">
        <p><strong>ChatGPT:</strong> Don't worry! Study Data Structures and Algorithms! Also, revisit common software design patterns! This can better help you conceptualize and plan your work!</p>
    </div>
</blockquote>

I can't find the actual conversation, as you may be able to tell (lol), but this was basically the gist.

So I devoted hours per day to structured learning. 

As a result, I'm a much more confident developer because:
- I know I can learn things I missed learning growing up (having taught myself to build websites without having studied computer science).
- I now have a documented, solid process for holistic problem solving.
- I have better foundational programming skills, data structure/algo knowledge, etc., which not only aid in my ability to perform front end tasks, but also help me gain footing in today's frameworks (many of which were written by people with traditional CS and lower-level programming backgrounds).

#### Closing thoughts

It's worth getting curious about the thing you'd like to learn, what your preconceived notions about it are, and what shame you feel around being "bad at the thing." 

Once you begin thinking of The Thing in a different light and make a plan to get started, you'll find that there are many more tools than ever before to help you on your way.

Best of luck Learning The Thing - you can do it! :)
